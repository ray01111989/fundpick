Understanding fund fees
Every fund charges a fee — but they're not all the same. Knowing what you're paying (and what's hidden) can save you thousands over time.
📊
Expense Ratio
Most important
0.03% – 1.5% / year
The annual fee the fund charges to manage your money. It's taken out automatically — you never write a check. Lower is always better.
Example: $10,000 invested at 0.03% = $3/year. At 1.0% = $100/year. Same investment, very different cost.
🏦
Broker Commission
Usually $0 now
$0 – $9.99 / trade
A fee your brokerage charges when you buy or sell a fund. Most major brokers (Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard, Robinhood) now charge $0 for ETFs and mutual funds.
What to do: Use Fidelity, Schwab, or Vanguard — all charge $0 commissions on the funds we recommend.
🚪
Load Fee
Avoid these
0% – 5.75% upfront or exit
Some old-school mutual funds charge a "load" — a sales commission paid when you buy (front-load) or sell (back-load). Modern index funds and ETFs never charge these.
Red flag: If a fund charges a load fee, look for a no-load alternative. You should never pay this for index investing.
↕️
Bid-Ask Spread
ETFs only — tiny
~0.01% – 0.1% per trade
ETFs trade like stocks, so there's a tiny gap between the buy and sell price. For large, popular ETFs like SPY or VTI this is negligible — fractions of a penny per share.
In practice: On a $10,000 ETF purchase, the spread costs you roughly $1–$10. Not worth worrying about.

Rule 1 — Under 0.2% is great. For index and ETF funds, aim for an expense ratio under 0.2%. Anything under 0.1% is excellent. The funds we recommend are almost always in this range.
✅
Rule 2 — No-load only. Never pay a front-load or back-load fee. All the funds we surface are no-load. If your advisor recommends a fund with a load, ask why.
✅
Rule 3 — Use a $0 commission broker. Fidelity, Schwab, and Vanguard all offer $0 trade commissions on ETFs and their own mutual funds. There's no reason to pay per trade anymore.
⚠️
Watch out — "no fee" isn't always free. Some brokers offer their own house funds with 0% expense ratios but make money other ways (cash sweeps, payment for order flow). These are still fine for most investors — just be aware.
